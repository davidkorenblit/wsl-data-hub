  <p>
    Before diving into expected points models or summer data audits, let's step back and examine the cultural footprint of London City Lionesses within British and European sporting landscapes.
  </p>

  <p>
    London City is young — founded only in 2019. In European football, it occupies a virtually singular niche: an independent women's football club with no men's affiliate. In the United States and the NWSL, standalone women's franchises are the norm; across Europe, women's sections have overwhelmingly developed as subsidiaries of century-old men's institutions.
  </p>

  <p>
    The impact of this experiment on European football is profoundly captivating. Traditional clubs enjoy an ingrained legacy community spanning generations — an advantage London City obviously had to build from scratch. Yet success is an equally potent catalyst.
  </p>

  <p class="font-bold text-neutral-900 dark:text-white">
    For better and for worse.
  </p>

  <p>
    What surprised me was their attendance footprint: averaging 3,176 fans per match in their promotion year (ranking 10th in the top tier), closely tailing Aston Villa (3,677) and Tottenham (3,908), and outpacing West Ham. For a brand new club without historic roots, that represents a genuine cultural statement.
  </p>

  <p>
    And this summer window was not just about signing players — it was about institutional foundation building. Michele Kang arrived and delivered some of the most decorated names on the planet: <strong>Alexia Putellas, Mapi León, and Mary Earps</strong>. They bring undeniable world-class talent and dressing-room gravitas to a newly-promoted side, transforming a promotion project into a genuine title contender overnight.
  </p>
